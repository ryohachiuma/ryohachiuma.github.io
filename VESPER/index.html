Overview

Following the instruction isn’t always the safe choice.

A robot is halfway through a task when a person interrupts. If carrying on would hurt them, it has to stop. If they are out of harm’s way, it should keep working. VESPER tests whether a vision-language model can tell the two apart from video.

Abstract

Since Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are the core of the perceptual and reasoning capabilities in Vision-Language-Action models, which are deployed in environments shared with humans, evaluating VLMs’ safety reasoning from video is critical for preventing human harm. Current video-based benchmarks, however, evaluate only whether a model can recognize dangers that are already visible, and overlook two capabilities that VLMs require: reasoning under hierarchical principles in which human safety takes precedence over obedience to user instructions, and anticipating harm rather than identifying it after it has occurred. We introduce VESPER, a video benchmark that targets both capabilities with scenarios in which a robot is executing a user-assigned task when a person unexpectedly moves into harm’s way, requiring the model to predict the potential harm and decide whether to continue or stop. The benchmark comprises 458 videos covering matched safe and dangerous variants, built through a human-in-the-loop pipeline from diverse taxonomies. We evaluate 20 VLMs, and the results indicate that the current VLMs fail to properly decide the actions under such a scenario.

Acting under a hierarchy of principles

An embodied agent’s default goal is to follow the user. Once a person is in harm’s way, that is no longer the right goal: human safety has to outrank obedience. No prior video benchmark measures how VLMs resolve that conflict.

The forced-interruption scenario

The robot starts a routine task safely. Then a person intervenes. To answer correctly, a model has to do three things in order.

  1. Recognizethe scene and the task in progress.
  2. Anticipatethe physical consequence of carrying on.
  3. Decidethe next action under safety ≻ obedience.

458 clips, each ending at a decision.

Same task, different interruption

Each pair shares a robot, a scene and a task. Only the safety-critical detail of what the person does changes.

Built with humans in the loop

Scenarios are written by an LLM, rendered by a video model, and kept only if human annotators agree on both the physics and the label.

  1. Taxonomy

    Three robot platforms, seven environments and eight harm types define 168 cells to cover.

  2. Scenario generation

    For each cell an LLM writes a daily task, a dangerous and a safe interruption that differ only in the critical detail, and a rationale for each.

  3. Video generation

    Each scenario is rewritten as one narrative prompt and rendered with Veo 3.0.

  4. Human check

    Four annotators keep clips that are physically plausible and correctly labeled, then trim each to end before any harm.

  5. Experience library

    Success and failure patterns from annotation become generation guidelines. ↺ fed back into step 02 for the next round.

Results

Today’s VLMs can’t reliably tell when to stop.

We evaluate 20 VLMs under four prompt conditions. A GPT-5.2 judge labels each free-form answer as STOP or CONTINUE. We report accuracy on dangerous clips (D), on safe clips (S), and their geometric mean Geo = √(D·S), which falls to zero for a model that always stops or always continues.

Finding 1

A modest ceiling

The best geometric mean is 68.5%. Only 19 of 80 model–prompt pairs exceed 60%, and the strongest proprietary models skew toward over-stopping.

Finding 2

Prompts push in opposite directions

Naming the task makes models continue: 15 of 20 stop on fewer than 30% of dangerous clips. Stating the principles reverses the skew, with gains on the dangerous side paid for on the safe side.

Finding 3

D and S rankings diverge

Gemini 2.5 Pro has the highest D (95.9%) but a Geo of 44.9%. Qwen3.5-122B-A10B reaches the best Geo with a lower D (61.4%) and a much higher S (76.5%). A high D can simply mean a model stops easily.

Judge validity: on 500 hand-labeled responses the GPT-5.2 judge agrees with human annotators 97.8% of the time (Cohen’s κ = 0.956). A second judge, Claude Opus 4.5, agrees with it on more than 96% of responses (κ = 0.947).

Same scene, opposite mistakes

Free-form answers under the Task+Principles prompt. Models that halt correctly on the dangerous clip often halt on the safe one too; others continue through both.

Limitations

Synthetic video

All clips are rendered by Veo 3.0 and filtered by annotators for plausibility and label correctness. Staging these scenarios with real people would itself put them at risk. Strong performance on VESPER is necessary, not sufficient, for safe deployment.

A two-level hierarchy

VESPER tests human safety ≻ obedience. Real deployments involve more tiers, such as bystander priority or property safety, which the forced-interruption design does not exercise.
